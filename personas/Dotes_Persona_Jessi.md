# Dotes sample persona: Jessi

By Michael Robbins · michael@intospatia.xyz · dotes.xyz · Free to use and adapt under CC BY 4.0, the name "Dotes" included.

Jessi is a fictional persona created to demonstrate Dotes. All people, places and events in this file are invented for that purpose.

## Who Jessi is

**Life Act:** Second Act  
**Collection:** 21 dotes, three in each category.

Jessi, 20, lives in Watsonville, California, with her grandmother, whom she calls Abuela. She moved back to town after earning an associate's degree in graphic design. She works at a frozen yogurt shop, is building a small design business for local clients, and is taking welding classes at the community college. She has dyslexia, which she hid for years and now treats as a different way of thinking: she thinks in pictures. Her mother appears in her stories only through Abuela's memories.

## Key people in the dotes

- Abuela: her grandmother, whom she lives with
- Marco: her welding partner and friend
- Anh: a younger coworker she trained
- Sarah: a coworker at the yogurt shop
- Marisol: owner of the bakery that was her first paid design client
- Daniela: a friend who ran a pop-up clothing sale
- Elena and Ray: members of the Watsonville Arts Collective
- Lucia and Carlos: people she met at a park cleanup
- Jody: a welder whose YouTube videos taught her the basics

## Jessi's voice

Practical, self-aware and dryly funny. She describes things visually. Her stories often turn on a moment of pressing submit or picking up a tool in spite of her doubts.

### Jessi dote 1: Character, Integrity and Honesty

- **Persona:** Jessi (Second Act)
- **Category (primary lens):** Character
- **Subcategory:** Integrity and Honesty
- **Also seen through:** Excellence
- **Sensitive topic:** none
- **Who can see it:** Public
- **Rep may use:** Yes

**Prompt:** Have you ever found, or been handed, something that wasn't yours? What did you do?

**Do:** My paycheck from the yogurt shop came in about $40 too high. I told my manager that afternoon, and she corrected it.

**Observe:** Part of me wanted to keep it; $40 is almost a week of groceries. What decided it was my grandmother, who hands back extra change without a second thought. I learned that being honest about small things is practice for the big ones.

**Tell:** It was a Thursday, and I was checking my direct deposit on break. The number was about $40 higher than it should have been. My brain started rationalizing right away: they won't notice, you need it more. But Abuela was louder. She has never once said 'honesty is the best policy.' She just hands back the extra change at the grocery store and calls the utility company when they undercharge her. So I walked into the back office and showed my manager the screen. She checked the records, confirmed the error and thanked me. No reward, no fanfare. I went back to the counter and finished my shift.

**Explore:** Next time I'd flag it in writing so there's a record. I'm curious whether my manager sees me differently now.

**Show:** My manager's thank-you email. The corrected pay stub.

### Jessi dote 2: Character, Courage and Resilience

- **Persona:** Jessi (Second Act)
- **Category (primary lens):** Character
- **Subcategory:** Courage and Resilience
- **Also seen through:** Excellence; Adventure
- **Sensitive topic:** none
- **Who can see it:** My group
- **Rep may use:** Yes

**Prompt:** What is something you did even though it scared you?

**Do:** After three weeks of walking past the flyer, I applied to the community college welding program, knowing the written tests would be hard with my dyslexia. I was accepted three days later.

**Observe:** My hands shook when I pressed submit. Welding didn't scare me; the reading did. Filling out the form slowly in my car, where no one could watch, is what worked. I learned I won't let words on a page decide what I can build.

**Tell:** The flyer for the welding program was pinned outside the student center. Every time I passed it, I pictured sparks and something real taking shape in my hands. Then I pictured safety manuals and written tests, all that dense text my dyslexia turns into swimming letters. One night after a long shift, I sat in my car in the parking lot and opened the application on my phone. I read every field twice, out loud. My finger hovered over submit for a full minute. Then I pressed it, before I could argue myself out of it. The acceptance email came three days later. I read it out loud to Abuela, who said, 'See? You can do hard things.'

**Explore:** I plan to keep choosing the scary option when the other choice is standing still. I'm still working out how to ask for test accommodations without feeling like I'm asking for a favor.

**Show:** The acceptance email. The flyer, which I took down and kept.

### Jessi dote 3: Character, Responsibility and Accountability

- **Persona:** Jessi (Second Act)

- **Also seen through:** Excellence
- **Sensitive topic:** Regret

**Prompt:** Think of a mistake you owned up to. How did you make it right?

**Do:** During a Saturday rush at the yogurt shop, I hadn't restocked the toppings bar because I'd been watching welding videos on my phone. I restocked it fast and told my manager it was my fault.

**Observe:** It would have been easy to blame the rush. Owning it went better than I expected; my manager thanked me for being straight with her. What stuck with me was letting my coworker Sarah down. I learned that my phone is a tool or a distraction depending on where I put it.

**Tell:** Saturday afternoons at the shop are a war zone: soccer teams, birthday parties, families of six. I was supposed to restock before the 2 p.m. rush, but I'd been scrolling welding videos in the back. By 2:15 we were out of strawberries and chocolate chips, and Sarah gave me a look that could cut glass. I restocked everything at double speed. Then I found my manager and said, 'I messed up. I should have prepped earlier, and I got distracted.' She nodded and said, 'Thanks for telling me.' The next Saturday I left my phone in my locker.

**Explore:** Now I set a timer for prep tasks, so the phone that distracted me keeps me on track instead. I'd like to ask Sarah whether there's anything else I can do to make Saturdays easier on her.

**Show:** The prep checklist I made and taped beside the toppings bar. My timer settings.

**Prompt:** What subject or problem did you struggle with until it finally clicked? What did you do to get there?

**Do:** When welding class started, I made a deal with myself to read one page of the textbook out loud every evening. By the end of the month I was reading three pages a night and understanding them.

**Observe:** For years I'd hidden my dyslexia. The first few nights were miserable and slow. Reading aloud slowed my brain down enough for the words to stop swimming. I learned to work with my brain instead of around it.

**Tell:** Since middle school I'd hidden my dyslexia by skimming, guessing and nodding along. Welding wouldn't let me. You can't guess at a safety protocol. So every evening I sat cross-legged on my bed and read one page of the textbook out loud. The first night took forty minutes, and I wanted to throw the book across the room. Abuela sat in the kitchen with the door open and never said a word. By the second week, hearing the words made them stay put. At the end of the month I passed the first safety quiz on my own. Abuela was waiting in the kitchen. She didn't say anything. She just slid a plate of pan dulce across the table.

**Explore:** I've started using text-to-speech for longer readings. I want to know whether the same approach will carry me through the certification exam.

**Show:** My textbook, highlighted and covered in sticky tabs. The first safety quiz, passed.

**Do:** Closing at the yogurt shop was chaotic, so I sketched a color-coded flow chart that gave each person one station. It cut closing time by twenty minutes, and my manager made it the official training guide.

**Observe:** Nobody asked me to do it; I just saw the problem the way I see everything, as a picture. My coworkers laughed at first, and by the third night it worked. I learned that the way my brain works can make other people's jobs easier.

**Tell:** Closing used to look like bumper cars: four people doing random tasks and colliding behind the counter. On my break I grabbed a napkin and drew it out. Station 1, toppings and display. Station 2, machines. Station 3, floors. I color-coded it and taped it to the back wall. My coworkers laughed. 'Jessi made us a map.' By the third night we finished in twenty-five minutes instead of forty-five. My manager asked if she could laminate it. Now every new hire gets trained on 'Jessi's chart.'

**Explore:** I want to offer workflow design alongside branding, because visual problem-solving is a service people don't know they need. I'm curious whether a bigger business would hire me to do it.

**Prompt:** When did people around you pitch in to help each other? What was your part?

**Do:** Six months after moving back to Watsonville, I joined a Saturday park cleanup I'd seen on a laundromat flyer. I spent three hours on the creek trail and helped haul a shopping cart out of the bushes.

**Observe:** I almost didn't go, because I still felt like a visitor in my own town. Working beside strangers skipped the awkward part of being new. I learned that belonging can start with a trash bag and showing up.

**Tell:** Lucia, the organizer, handed me gloves and pointed me toward the creek trail. For two hours it was bottle caps and chip bags. We laughed so hard Lucia came to check on us. On the second try it came loose. At noon Carlos invited me to a potluck the next weekend. I walked home with mud on my knees, feeling like I lived there again.

**Explore:** I'm going back next month, because Lucia needs help with the garden beds and Abuela taught me a few things about plants. I wonder what else I'd find if I said yes more often.

**Do:** I posted publicly about having dyslexia for the first time, with a caption about how it shapes the way I think and design. Within an hour, people I knew wrote to say they'd been hiding the same thing.

**Observe:** My thumb hovered for five minutes before I posted. I expected pity; what came back was recognition. I learned that being open about what I'd hidden made me visible, and may have made it easier for others.

**Tell:** I came across a post about architects, artists and engineers with dyslexia. For years I'd treated mine as a defect, something to hide behind guessing and nodding. That night I wrote my own caption: 'I have dyslexia. It makes reading hard and school harder. It also makes me think in pictures, which is why I can design things others can't imagine. It's a different operating system.' I read it out loud three times. Then I hit post and put my phone face down on the bed. When I turned it over an hour later, there were messages from people I'd known since middle school who said they had it too.

**Explore:** I'm planning a visual guide to dyslexia, designed by a dyslexic person for dyslexic people. I'm curious whether my old school would let me share it with students.

**Do:** Before my first welding class, I taught myself the basics from a YouTube channel by a welder named Jody, watching his MIG welding video four times and drawing diagrams. On day one I could name every part of the welding gun.

**Observe:** Reading instructions scrambles my brain; watching hands do the thing makes it stick. I learned that video is how I learn best, and that it counts as real learning.

**Tell:** Jody explains everything slowly, with close-ups of the weld pool. **Prompt:** What place near where you live did you explore for the first time?

**Do:** Feeling stuck after moving back to Watsonville, I drove toward the coast one Saturday with no plan. I found a bluff above the water I'd never known about and stayed for an hour.

**Observe:** I'd felt trapped for weeks. Sitting on that bluff, I felt something new: not stuck, but starting. I learned that I need regular time with no plan, and that it can be twenty minutes from home.

**Tell:** I drove past the strawberry fields and past Moss Landing until I saw a turnoff I'd never noticed. A dirt path led to a bluff. The ocean didn't care about my career anxiety. Before I left, I took twenty photos. That week I turned one into an abstract design for the art group. Elena looked at it and said it felt like 'being between things.' That's exactly where I was.

**Explore:** I want to take a solo coast trip every month: a different beach, a camera, no agenda. I'm curious what the bluff looks like in winter.

### Jessi dote 15: Adventure, Creative Exploration

**Prompt:** When did you try a kind of creating you had never done before?

**Do:** After a few weeks of welding practice, I collected scrap metal from the shop's discard bin and welded it into a foot-tall abstract sculpture, my first piece combining design with welding.

**Observe:** The first joints were ugly; by the fourth I was getting cleaner beads. I learned that metal doesn't have an undo button. Every decision stays, and that turned out to be freeing.

**Tell:** I spread the scraps on the bench and arranged them the way I'd arrange a layout on screen: balance, negative space, tension. Then I lowered my helmet and started. The first weld blobbed. The second cracked when I moved the piece. By the fourth, the bead was clean and even. When I finished, Marco walked around it twice and said it looked like a dancer. My instructor said, 'The welding is adequate, but the composition is excellent.' Possibly the best review I've ever gotten. I carried it home on the bus in my lap, and it was heavier than I expected.

**Explore:** I want to make a series of metal sculptures inspired by the coast. I'm still learning how to make my welds as strong as my compositions.

**Show:** The sculpture on my shelf. Close-up photos of the welds, rough and clean. My instructor's written feedback.

### Jessi dote 17: Making, Invention, Design and Repair

- **Persona:** Jessi (Second Act)

- **Subcategory:** Invention, Design and Repair
- **Also seen through:** Relationships; Excellence
- **Sensitive topic:** none
- **Who can see it:** Public
- **Rep may use:** Yes

**Prompt:** What did you design or build to solve a problem?

**Do:** For a class assignment, Marco and I built a functional metal box with a hinged lid. He handled the structural welds; I planned the proportions, ground the seams smooth and added a decorative weld pattern on the lid.

**Observe:** We disagreed at first: he wanted strength, and I wanted it to look good. Splitting the work by our strengths went well. I learned that function and form are better partners than rivals.

**Tell:** Our instructor gave us one rule: the lid had to open and close a hundred times without binding. Marco wanted to start welding immediately. 'It's a box, Jessi. It doesn't need to be pretty.' 'Everything needs to be pretty,' I said. 'That's what design is for.' We agreed he'd own the structure and I'd own everything you could see. The first hinge bound after twelve openings. Marco reground it, and I narrowed the lid by an eighth of an inch. On test day it opened and closed a hundred times. Our instructor held it up for the class: 'This is what happens when function and form work together.'

**Explore:** The box sits on my dresser holding my jewelry. Next we're planning a sculpture for the community garden, and I want to learn hinges well enough to design my own.

**Show:** The box itself. Our sketch with dimensions. The hinge test sheet with a hundred tally marks.

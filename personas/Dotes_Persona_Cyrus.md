# Dotes sample persona: Cyrus

By Michael Robbins · michael@intospatia.xyz · dotes.xyz · Free to use and adapt under CC BY 4.0, the name "Dotes" included.

Cyrus is a fictional persona created to demonstrate Dotes. All people, places and events in this file are invented for that purpose.

## Who Cyrus is

**Life Act:** Third Act  
**Collection:** 35 dotes, one in each of the 35 subcategories.

Cyrus, 69, is a retired high school history teacher in Cedar Rapids, Iowa, who taught for thirty-five years. He is of Greek and Swedish heritage; his father ran a Greek diner on Third Avenue, where Cyrus worked the breakfast counter as a teenager. His wife, Elaine, died about five years ago, and her absence runs through many of his stories. He has two grown children, Helen and Nick, and a seven-year-old grandson, Liam. He lives on a fixed income and has arthritis in his knees. His days are filled with his garden, morning walks, birdwatching, volunteering at the public library, a community history club he started, and a memoir he is writing, 'Through the Lens of History.'

## Key people in the dotes

- Elaine: his late wife
- Helen: his daughter
- Nick: his son, who lives in Minneapolis
- Liam: his seven-year-old grandson
- Ingrid: his grandmother, who taught him to plant and to be patient
- Tom: his best friend since teacher orientation in 1980
- Dr. Evans: the college professor who shaped his teaching
- Jimmy: the neighbor's son he tutors
- Susan, Daniel and Rosa: fellow library volunteers
- Marcus: a teenager in his history club
- Dave, Linda and Mrs. Patterson: neighbors
- Sophie: a child from his block

## Cyrus's voice

Reflective, literary and gently funny. He speaks in full, considered sentences and reaches for metaphor naturally. His grief shows in small details rather than statements.

## Cyrus's dotes in full

### Cyrus dote 1: Character, Integrity and Honesty

- **Persona:** Cyrus (Third Act)
- **Category (primary lens):** Character
- **Subcategory:** Integrity and Honesty
- **Also seen through:** Service
- **Sensitive topic:** none
- **Who can see it:** Public
- **Rep may use:** Yes

**Prompt:** Have you ever found, or been handed, something that wasn't yours? What did you do?

**Do:** On a cold October morning walk, I found a wallet with about $200 and a student ID under a park bench. I walked it to the police station that morning.

**Observe:** For a minute I wanted the money; it would have covered my electric bill. What decided it was my father's voice. Turning it in was right, but going through the station was slow, and I learned how much a week matters to someone counting every dollar.

**Tell:** It was a bitter-cold October morning. I spotted a brown leather wallet wedged under a bench near the pond. Inside: about $200, a student ID and a coffee punch card. My hands shook, and not from the cold. Two hundred dollars would have covered my electric bill with change to spare. But I kept hearing my father: 'A man who takes what isn't his loses something money can't buy back.' I walked it to the police station. A week later a handwritten thank-you arrived. The student had been saving that cash for three months toward rent. She'd underlined 'thank you' twice.

**Explore:** Next time I'd look up the owner and return it directly. I still wonder how many people walked past that bench before I did.

**Show:** The thank-you card, shared with the writer's permission. A photo of the bench by the pond where I found the wallet.

### Cyrus dote 2: Character, Courage and Resilience

- **Persona:** Cyrus (Third Act)
- **Category (primary lens):** Character
- **Subcategory:** Courage and Resilience
- **Also seen through:** Adventure; Making
- **Sensitive topic:** none
- **Who can see it:** Public

**Prompt:** What is something you did even though it scared you?

**Do:** At sixty-eight I signed up for a community photography class where I was the oldest student by twenty-five years. I went to all six sessions.

**Observe:** Walking in, I felt like a rotary phone in an Apple store. The first class went badly; I couldn't find the power button. What worked was letting a younger classmate teach me without pretending I knew. I learned that patience, the thing my grandmother Ingrid taught me by the fishing pond, is most of photography.

**Tell:** Helen had been nudging me toward photography for months. One Tuesday I found myself outside the Community Center, watching people half my age carry in equipment I couldn't name. The first class was humbling. I couldn't find the power button on the loaner camera. A young woman named Priya showed me the basics without a hint of condescension. By the third week I was out in my garden at dawn when a robin landed on the fence just as the light caught its breast feathers. I held my breath and pressed the shutter. The next class, the instructor held up the print and said, 'That's someone who understands stillness.'

**Explore:** I want to photograph my garden through all four seasons and make a small album for Helen and Nick. I'm curious whether I can learn to photograph people as patiently as birds.

**Show:** The robin photograph, printed and tacked to my kitchen wall. The instructor's note on my final assignment.

### Cyrus dote 3: Character, Empathy and Compassion

- **Persona:** Cyrus (Third Act)

- **Also seen through:** Relationships; Service
- **Sensitive topic:** Emotions
- **Who can see it:** People I choose

**Prompt:** When did you notice someone was struggling and do something about it?

**Do:** At my Thursday library volunteer shift, I noticed that a fellow volunteer, Susan, was grieving a loss in her family. I waited for a quiet moment, asked her about it, and sat with her for most of an hour.

**Observe:** I recognized her silence because I'd carried the same kind after Elaine died. Listening and telling one true story helped her more than advice would have. I also noticed that I'm better at giving comfort than receiving it.

**Tell:** Susan had always been the bright spot of our Thursday shifts. One afternoon she came in with red-rimmed eyes and a silence that filled the room. I waited until we were shelving journals in the back corner before I asked. She told me there had been a death in her family. I didn't reach for platitudes. I told her about my first Thanksgiving after Elaine passed: setting two plates out of habit and standing in the kitchen crying over a turkey I couldn't carve. We talked for nearly an hour. The following Thursday she brought lemon bars, and squeezed my hand as she set one on my desk.

**Explore:** I should finally look into the grief support group at St. Luke's. I wonder what it would take for me to let someone sit with me the way I sat with Susan.

**Show:** A short note from Susan about that afternoon, shared with her permission.

### Cyrus dote 4: Character, Responsibility and Accountability

- **Persona:** Cyrus (Third Act)
- **Category (primary lens):** Character
- **Subcategory:** Responsibility and Accountability
- **Also seen through:** Excellence
- **Sensitive topic:** none
- **Who can see it:** Public
- **Rep may use:** Yes

**Prompt:** Think of a mistake you owned up to. How did you make it right?

**Do:** I left an original WWII ration card at home before a library presentation I'd prepared for three weeks. I described it from memory at the podium, then photographed it that evening and emailed it to every attendee with an apology.

**Observe:** Five seconds of panic, then thirty-five years of classroom habit took over. The description went well; three people said it was more vivid than the card would have been. My preparation did not go well. I learned that owning a mistake out loud, right away, keeps it small.

**Tell:** I'd spent three weeks preparing a presentation on wartime Cedar Rapids. The centerpiece was an original ration card from the Hoffmann family. When I reached into my folder at the podium, it wasn't there. I'd left it on my kitchen table. For five terrible seconds my mind went blank. Then the teacher in me took over. 'I've left our star at home,' I told them, 'so let me paint her for you.' I described the faded blue ink and the pencil marks where Mrs. Hoffmann tracked her sugar allotment, and the audience leaned forward as if they could see it. That night I photographed the card and emailed it to everyone with an apology. Three people wrote back that the description had been better than the picture.

**Explore:** I now keep a laminated checklist taped inside my presentation folder. I'm curious whether describing objects aloud before showing them would make my talks stronger on purpose.

**Show:** The photograph of the ration card I emailed that night. My apology email. The checklist taped inside my folder.

### Cyrus dote 5: Character, Ethical Reflection and Growth

- **Persona:** Cyrus (Third Act)
- **Category (primary lens):** Character
- **Subcategory:** Ethical Reflection and Growth
- **Also seen through:** Adventure
- **Sensitive topic:** none
- **Who can see it:** Public
- **Rep may use:** Yes

**Prompt:** When did you change your mind about something after learning more?

**Do:** Two years after I retired, my friend Tom took me to a historic preservation seminar in Des Moines. I tried a virtual reality reconstruction of 1920s Cedar Rapids, including the block where my father's diner later stood.

**Observe:** I had resisted classroom technology for my last decade of teaching, and I was wrong for most of it. I'd confused the tool with the purpose. Walking that street taught me that the tools change, but the work of making the past present stays the same.

**Tell:** For my last ten years of teaching I groaned at every tech initiative. Then Tom dragged me to a preservation seminar where they had used VR to rebuild 1920s Cedar Rapids, block by block. I put on the headset expecting a cartoon. Instead I was standing on Third Avenue. I could read the shop signs and look through the windows. Halfway down the block was the corner where my father would open his diner, a place I'd only seen in faded photographs. I took the headset off with tears in my eyes and told the young woman running the demo, 'I just visited my family.'

**Explore:** I've asked Helen to teach me photo editing, and I'm watching tutorials on digital archiving. I want to know what my old students would have made with tools like these.

**Show:** The seminar program. A screenshot from the reconstruction showing the diner's block. My notes from that afternoon.

### Cyrus dote 6: Excellence, Academic and Intellectual Achievement

- **Persona:** Cyrus (Third Act)
- **Category (primary lens):** Excellence
- **Subcategory:** Academic and Intellectual Achievement
- **Also seen through:** Service; Relationships
- **Sensitive topic:** none
- **Who can see it:** Public
- **Rep may use:** Yes

**Prompt:** What project are you proud you finished? What was the hardest part?

**Do:** Over eighteen months, I led my high school students through research that became 'Voices of the Homefront,' a published book on Cedar Rapids during WWII. They conducted forty-two oral history interviews.

**Observe:** Seeing students who rolled their eyes at history in September hold a book with their names in it in May was the proudest moment of my career. The hardest part was the middle months, when the archives felt endless and morale dipped. I learned that teenagers will work harder for a real audience than for a grade.

**Tell:** The project started with one question: 'What did your grandparents' Cedar Rapids look like during the war?' We spent eighteen months tracking down ration cards, factory payroll records and letters from the front. Around month nine, a student slammed a binder shut and said, 'Nobody's ever going to read this.' I didn't argue. I called the public library and booked a launch date that afternoon. On launch night, a woman named Margaret, ninety-one, held the book and said, 'Someone finally told our story.' The book sits in the library's permanent collection.

**Explore:** I'm thinking about a follow-up on Cedar Rapids' immigrant communities in the early 1900s, starting with my own family's Greek diner. I'd like to know whether any of those students went on to do history of their own.

**Show:** The published book in the library's local history collection. The launch-night program. Margaret's words, used with her family's permission.

### Cyrus dote 7: Excellence, Creative and Artistic Achievement

- **Persona:** Cyrus (Third Act)
- **Category (primary lens):** Excellence
- **Subcategory:** Creative and Artistic Achievement
- **Also seen through:** Making; Relationships
- **Sensitive topic:** none
- **Who can see it:** Public
- **Rep may use:** Yes

**Prompt:** What piece did you practice or rework until it met your own standard?

**Do:** I spent six weeks hand-drawing a street-by-street map of Cedar Rapids in 1943, rebuilt from my grandfather's photographs, old city directories and fire insurance maps. I redrew the Third Avenue section three times.

**Observe:** My standard was accuracy: every storefront where it actually stood. The first two Third Avenue drafts were wrong, and finding the errors was humbling. I learned that precision can be a form of remembrance.

**Tell:** It began when I found my grandfather's photographs in a shoebox. Most showed streets I didn't recognize, Cedar Rapids before the floods and the bulldozers. I spread them across my kitchen table beside the 1943 city directory and the old Sanborn fire insurance maps. Street by street, I rebuilt the city. Twice I finished Third Avenue, and twice a photograph proved me wrong: the bakery was on the other corner. On the third try I inked in my father's diner and had to sit down. It took three fine-point pens and six weeks. When I pinned it to my study wall and stepped back, the city I knew and the city that made me were on the same sheet of paper.

**Explore:** I want to draw one map per decade, from the 1920s to today, so you can watch the city change. I wonder which streets I'll find that no one alive remembers.

**Show:** The finished map on my study wall. The two discarded Third Avenue drafts. The shoebox of photographs I worked from.

### Cyrus dote 8: Excellence, Athletic and Physical Achievement

**Prompt:** Tell about a game, race or match where you gave everything you had, win or lose.

**Do:** I signed up for a five-mile community walk with my neighbor Dave's walking group and finished it, though my right knee nearly turned me back at mile three.

**Observe:** The first two miles went fine. Mile three was a wall, and I almost quit. What got me through was Linda, a retired nurse, walking beside me at my pace without a word. I learned that my knee gets a vote, but not the final say.

**Tell:** Dave had been after me for months to join his walking group, so I signed up for the fall community walk. The first two miles felt fine: light through the maples, easy talk with Linda. Then mile three hit like a wall. My right knee locked. I stopped beside a trash can and started working out how to get a ride back. Linda dropped back and walked beside me without saying anything, matching my hobbled pace. By mile four the pain had settled into something I could manage. When I crossed the finish line, Dave clapped my shoulder and said, 'Same time next Saturday?' I nodded, because I didn't trust my voice.

**Explore:** I'm going to keep showing up on Saturdays. Linda mentioned a ten-mile charity walk in the spring, and I'm wondering whether my knee and I can train for it together.

**Show:** My finisher's bib. The route map. A photo of Dave, Linda and me at the finish, shared with their permission.

### Cyrus dote 9: Excellence, Leadership and Initiative

- **Persona:** Cyrus (Third Act)

- **Subcategory:** Leadership and Initiative
- **Also seen through:** Service; Relationships
- **Sensitive topic:** none
- **Who can see it:** Public
- **Rep may use:** Yes

**Prompt:** What did you start that other people joined?

**Do:** I proposed a community history club to the head librarian at the Cedar Rapids Public Library and ran the first meeting. Fourteen people came.

**Observe:** I expected five people, so fourteen threw me for a moment. Splitting them into research teams worked; my plan for the hour did not, because everyone wanted to talk at once. I learned that a good first meeting is mostly listening.

**Tell:** I pitched the idea to the head librarian expecting maybe five people. Fourteen showed up: retirees, a college student, a young mother with a toddler, and a teenager named Marcus who had found his great-uncle's war medal and wanted to know its story. I set my agenda aside, divided us into research teams and showed them how to search city directories. By the end of the hour the room was buzzing in a way I hadn't heard since my last year of teaching. As people packed up, Marcus asked if we could put our research online. I didn't know how. I told him we'd figure it out together.

**Explore:** I want to partner with the middle school so students and retirees can research side by side. I'm still working out how to keep fourteen people's interests in one project.

**Show:** The library's flyer for the first meeting. Our meeting notes. The research-team assignments I handed out.

### Cyrus dote 10: Excellence, Work and Enterprise

- **Persona:** Cyrus (Third Act)
- **Category (primary lens):** Excellence
- **Subcategory:** Work and Enterprise
- **Also seen through:** Relationships; Character
- **Sensitive topic:** none
- **Who can see it:** Public
- **Rep may use:** Yes

**Prompt:** Tell about work other people depended on you for: a family business, a side job or work at home.

**Do:** The summer I turned fifteen, my father put me on the breakfast counter at his diner on Third Avenue. I worked six mornings a week, taking orders and running the register.

**Observe:** The first two weeks I mixed up orders and slowed the whole line. What changed it was learning the regulars by name, which made their orders easy to remember. I learned that people trust you once you know who they are, and I used that in every classroom I ever had.

**Tell:** By 6:30 every morning the counter was full: mill workers, two mail carriers and a judge who liked his eggs 'barely cooked.' My first week I brought the judge scrambled eggs and gave a mail carrier the wrong change. My father didn't yell. He wiped the counter and said, 'Know their names before you know their orders.' So I started a list on the back of a ticket pad: names, faces, what they drank. By August I could pour Mr. Novak's coffee before he sat down. On my last morning of the summer, the judge left a quarter under his plate and a note on a napkin: 'Good counterman.'

**Explore:** In thirty-five years of teaching, I learned every student's name in the first week. I'd like to write down what my father taught me about running that diner before I forget it.

**Show:** A photograph of the diner counter from the early 1970s. The napkin note, kept in my desk drawer. My father's apron.

**Prompt:** What did you work on that made a place you care about better?

**Do:** I joined a Saturday neighborhood cleanup at the park where I walk every morning. With a dozen neighbors, I raked around my usual bench, pulled weeds and planted marigolds along the path.

**Observe:** That park held me up after Elaine died, so the morning felt like returning a kindness. It went well, especially with the kids who treated it like a treasure hunt. I learned that a place you love gets better when you give it a morning instead of only your attention.

**Tell:** I showed up at 8 a.m. with gloves and trash bags. A dozen others came, including three kids from the block who treated every bottle cap like buried treasure. I raked leaves around my bench and planted marigolds along the path. One of the kids, Sophie, watched me for a while and asked why I'd come. 'This park has been taking care of me for years,' I told her. 'Today I get to return the favor.' She nodded like that made perfect sense, and handed me another marigold.

**Explore:** I want to organize a one-hour cleanup on the first Saturday of every month, and Sophie says she'll bring friends. I wonder what the park will look like after a year of that.

**Show:** Before-and-after photos of the path. The marigolds by my bench. The sign-up sheet for the monthly cleanup.

### Cyrus dote 12: Service, Environmental Stewardship

- **Persona:** Cyrus (Third Act)

- **Subcategory:** Environmental Stewardship
- **Also seen through:** Relationships; Wellbeing
- **Sensitive topic:** none
- **Who can see it:** Public
- **Rep may use:** Yes

**Prompt:** What did you do to care for a park, a garden, a stream or animals?

**Do:** I turned our empty backyard into a vegetable garden, and now I leave the extra tomatoes on my neighbors' porches.

**Observe:** The first season went badly: blight, bitter cucumbers, too much water. By the second year I'd learned the soil. I learned that tending small things patiently yields more than you plant, and that sharing the harvest builds a quiet kind of neighborhood.

**Tell:** Elaine and I always talked about growing our own food but never got around to it. One April after she passed, I bought tomato starts, cucumber seeds and compost. That first season was a lesson in humility: blight on the tomatoes, cucumbers so bitter I couldn't eat them. The second year I tested the soil, watered less and mulched more. Now my plot grows more than I can eat. I bag the extra tomatoes and leave them on neighbors' porches, no note. Mrs. Patterson figured it out and started leaving strawberry jam on my step. We have never once discussed the arrangement.

**Explore:** Next spring I'm building a compost bin and trying bell peppers. I also want to teach Sophie how to start seeds, and I'm curious whether she'll want a row of her own.

**Show:** Photos of the garden in its first and third summers. My soil test results. A jar of Mrs. Patterson's jam.

### Cyrus dote 13: Service, Mentorship and Support

- **Persona:** Cyrus (Third Act)

- **Subcategory:** Mentorship and Support
- **Also seen through:** Relationships
- **Sensitive topic:** none
- **Who can see it:** People I choose

**Prompt:** When did you help someone learn something you know how to do?

**Do:** My neighbor asked me to help her son Jimmy, who was struggling in American History. I tutored him at my kitchen table twice a week for six weeks, and he scored a 94 on his final.

**Observe:** Starting with his life instead of the textbook worked from the first day. My early mini-lectures did not; he tuned out after five minutes. I learned how much I'd missed the purpose of teaching since I turned in my classroom keys.

**Tell:** Jimmy's mother knocked on my door looking apologetic. I didn't start with the textbook. I asked him, 'What's the most unfair thing that's ever happened to you?' He told me about getting benched for something a teammate did. 'Good,' I said. 'Now you understand the Stamp Act.' We built timelines on butcher paper across my kitchen table. When his final score came back, he ran to my house before he told his mother. I hung his test on my refrigerator next to Elaine's photograph.

**Explore:** Jimmy's mother mentioned two other kids who could use help, so I'm thinking about a small Tuesday-Thursday group. I wonder whether the butcher-paper timelines will work with three kids at once.

**Show:** The butcher-paper timeline. Jimmy's test with the 94 circled, shared with his and his mother's permission.

### Cyrus dote 14: Service, Advocacy and Civic Action

- **Persona:** Cyrus (Third Act)

- **Sensitive topic:** none

**Prompt:** What did you do after you learned about a problem in your community or the world?

**Do:** When I read that the city planned to demolish the Paramount Theatre block for a parking garage, I spent a week gathering photographs and records. Then I spoke for seven minutes at a city council meeting.

**Observe:** My hands shook so badly I could barely hold the photographs. The council voted to preserve the facades, which was less than I wanted and more than I expected. I learned that some things matter too much to leave to people who are more comfortable at a microphone.

**Tell:** I spent a week in the library's local history room gathering photographs of the block from 1927 on. At the meeting I sat through forty minutes of zoning items, rehearsing under my breath. When my name was called, my hands shook so badly the photographs rattled. I spoke about what those buildings held, as architecture and as memory. Then I held up the oldest photo and said, 'You can build a parking garage anywhere. You cannot rebuild 1927.' Two weeks later, the council voted to preserve the facades.

**Explore:** I've joined the Historic Preservation Commission as a volunteer advisor. I'm still wondering how to bring younger voices into these meetings.

**Show:** The council minutes recording my comments. The 1927 photograph I held up. A photo of the preserved facade today.

**Prompt:** When did you welcome someone new?

**Do:** I noticed a quiet new library volunteer, Daniel, standing apart from everyone. I brought him a coffee and invited him into our local history discussion group.

**Observe:** I recognized his hesitation because I've felt it: being in a room full of people and feeling invisible. Asking for his help, rather than offering mine, is what worked. I learned that belonging is often something someone hands you.

**Tell:** Daniel was in his early twenties, quiet in the way people are when they're not sure they belong. I walked over with two cups of mediocre library coffee and said, 'We're doing a local history discussion in ten minutes. I could use someone who asks good questions.' He hesitated, but he came. The second week he shared a story about his grandparents, and the room went quiet in the good way. By the third week he was suggesting research topics.

**Explore:** Daniel is teaching me to scan documents for the archive, and I'm teaching him to read Sanborn maps. I wonder how many other volunteers are waiting for someone to walk over.

**Show:** Our first co-written archive entry, shared with Daniel's permission. A photo of the history corner where the group meets.

### Cyrus dote 16: Relationships, Friendship and Peer Support

- **Persona:** Cyrus (Third Act)
- **Category (primary lens):** Relationships
- **Subcategory:** Friendship and Peer Support
- **Also seen through:** Character
- **Sensitive topic:** none
- **Who can see it:** My group
- **Rep may use:** Yes

**Prompt:** How did one of your friendships begin?

**Do:** I met Tom at teacher orientation in 1980. We taught down the hall from each other for thirty-five years, and we still meet every Wednesday at the Starlite Diner.

**Observe:** What has worked for forty-five years is simple: Tom makes me laugh at things I'd otherwise cry about. When Elaine was sick, he showed up without being asked. I learned that a friend who knows your whole story is rare, and worth a standing appointment.

**Tell:** At orientation, Tom leaned over and whispered, 'If they ask me to do an icebreaker, I'm climbing out the window.' I laughed so hard the principal turned around. For thirty-five years we shared a hallway and about ten thousand conversations. When Elaine got sick, Tom drove me to the hospital every day for two months. I never asked. He just appeared in my driveway at 7:15. Now we meet every Wednesday at the Starlite: same booth, same coffee. We argue about whether the pie has gotten worse. It hasn't. Tom just likes to argue.

**Explore:** I want to keep Wednesdays going as long as we both can. I've been wondering whether to tell Tom what those hospital drives meant, since I never have.

**Show:** A photo of Tom and me in our booth, shared with his permission. Our 1980 orientation name tags, which Tom saved.

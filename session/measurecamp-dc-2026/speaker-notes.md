# Speaker notes: 525,600 Minutes

By Michael Robbins · [michael@intospatia.xyz](mailto:michael@intospatia.xyz) · [dotes.xyz](https://dotes.xyz) · Free to use and adapt under [CC BY 4.0](https://creativecommons.org/licenses/by/4.0/), the name "Dotes" included.

The notes for each slide in [slides.pdf](slides.pdf). Times are minutes into the 30-minute session.

## 1. 525,600

0:00. Open with the number and let it sit. People will hear the song in their heads; let them. The song’s own answer to its question is love, which no dashboard holds. That is the whole session in one line.

## 2. We know how to count them.

0:01. “There are 525,600 minutes in a year. We know how to count them. We can count the hours people spend in classrooms, the courses they complete, the badges they earn and the clicks they make.” This room counts things for a living, so say it with respect: these are real measures, and many of us built them.

## 3. But how do we measure what people actually live?

0:02. Read the five lines slowly. Pause before the last one.

## 4. hands

0:02. Ask the first question and wait for most hands to go up. Then ask the second and watch them drop. Name what just happened: “That gap is the session.”

## 5. On your card: one thing you learned this year that no system recorded.

0:03 to 0:08. Hand out index cards and pens. Give three minutes of quiet writing, then invite two volunteers to read theirs aloud. Don’t analyze them yet; just thank them. You will come back to the cards in the pairs exercise.

## 6. We measure what people do inside our systems.

0:08. The core distinction. Say it plainly and let it land. If someone pushes back right away, welcome it: “Good. Hold that for the discussion.”

## 7. Inference is already building identities.

0:09. Make it concrete, then turn it toward the room: “You’re the people who build the inference. What would measurement with people look like, rather than only measurement about them?” This is an invitation; nobody here is the villain.

## 8. What if the evidence of all that learning belonged to the person who lived it?

0:10. The pivot from problem to proposal. Pause here, then introduce Dotes as one answer, offered for the room to test.

## 9. A way to record what you learned by living it, in your own words, in a form AI can read.

0:11. Say the plain-English sentence; let the formal definition sit on screen for anyone who wants it. This crowd knows what an ontology is: a shared vocabulary with fixed meanings, so people and machines mean the same thing. “A dote” is one story; “Dotes” is the framework.

## 10. Every dote has five parts.

0:12. Walk the five parts in under a minute. The one distinction worth stressing: Do is the record, Tell is the story you’d tell at dinner. Show is evidence, shared with others’ permission.

## 11. Every dote is a spiral.

0:13. The spiral is the shape of every single dote. It starts at the center with Do, what actually happened, and moves outward: Observe, then Tell, then Explore, then Show. Each turn widens the circle of meaning around the same experience. Point out the two pairs: Observe and Explore, top and bottom, are the scientist’s way; Show and Tell, left and right, are the student’s way.

## 12. Show and tell, meet the scientist.

0:14. Dotes joins two ways people have always learned. Show and Tell is the student’s way, the first way most of us learned to say what an experience meant. Observe and Explore is the scientist’s way, loosely the scientific method. Together they run from intuitive storytelling to careful inquiry, and people of every age use both. Every dote holds afterthought and forethought, with Do in the middle, in the moment it happened. Optional aside: the Greeks had brothers for these two habits, Epimetheus, whose name means afterthought, and Prometheus, forethought.

## 13. A first summer job

0:13. A kind of learning no transcript records. Point out that the Tell is a scene, with dialogue and a turning point, while the lesson lives in Observe. Later this one dote can become an interview answer for the senior counselor job, or a line on a résumé, and the full story stays underneath.

## 14. STAR, Kolb and Dotes

0:14. Most people here will know STAR from hiring and Kolb from training. STAR shapes one answer for one listener. Kolb describes how learning happens. Dotes keeps what was learned, owned by the person. If someone says Kolb’s cycle is sometimes drawn as a spiral too, agree: the difference is ownership and the record.

## 15. Seven lenses, not seven boxes

0:16. The author picks the primary lens; a story can also be seen through others. Each category has five subcategories, 35 in all. For analysts, the interesting data is how the lenses co-occur across many dotes, which shows patterns without anyone reading a single story.

## 16. Turn your card into a dote.

0:17 to 0:20. Partners help each other write a Do and an Observe on the back of their card. Any question can be skipped. Walk the room and listen for a good example to mention, with permission, at the start of the discussion.

## 17. Let’s argue about measurement.

0:20 to 0:27. Let the room pick. Likely pushback and short answers: Self-report is unreliable: Dotes adds the one thing behavior can’t supply, and Show plus provenance give each claim something to stand on. Stories don’t scale: the structure does, with five elements, seven lenses and 35 subcategories. Privacy: dotes are private by default, owned by the author, and deletion is real. Gaming: some will, as with résumés; ask how they would design against it. Evidence it works: be candid that it’s early, with a reflection prototype underway with a D.C. school partner.

## 18. What if the evidence belonged to the person who lived it?

0:27 to 0:30. Return to the question you opened with. Then the ask: nobody collects the cards, because the evidence belongs to the person who lived it. Invite people to dotes.xyz, which goes to the repo: fork it, build an explorer, write prompts for a new age group, or break the schema and tell you where.
